# Best Productivity Resources for 2026: 15 Tools, Apps, and Skills That Help You Get More Done

**Last updated: October 7, 2026**

Productivity is not about filling your phone or browser with dozens of apps. It is about removing the specific things that slow you down: forgotten tasks, scattered notes, poor scheduling, distractions, repetitive work, unclear priorities, and inefficient computer habits.

This curated list of **productivity resources** focuses on practical tools and skills for **students, developers, freelancers, remote workers, writers, creators, teachers, and small teams**. Each resource has a clear job, so you can choose based on the problem you actually need to solve.

> **Quick idea:** Do not install everything on this list. Pick one resource for your biggest bottleneck, build a habit around it, and add another only when a new problem appears.

## 1. Todoist — capture and organize the work that matters

A good task manager should make it easier to get commitments out of your head and into a system you can trust. **Todoist** is a strong choice for personal task management, recurring tasks, deadlines, projects, and everyday planning.

It works well for students managing assignments, freelancers managing client work, and professionals who need a simple place to capture the next action.

<a href="https://todoist.com/" >Visit Todoist</a>

## 2. Notion — keep notes, documents, and projects together

When information is spread across random documents, chats, bookmarks, and notebooks, productivity often suffers before the actual work even begins. **Notion** can act as a central workspace for notes, project information, documentation, meeting notes, checklists, and personal knowledge.

It is especially useful for people who want one flexible system rather than separate tools for every type of information.

<a href="https://www.notion.com/" rel="nofollow">Visit Notion</a>

## 3. Trello — visualize work with a simple workflow

**Trello** is useful when you want to see work move from one stage to another. A basic board might use columns such as **Backlog → In Progress → Review → Done**.

That visual model can work for software projects, content calendars, study plans, personal goals, marketing tasks, and small-team workflows.

<a href="https://trello.com/" rel="nofollow">Visit Trello</a>

## 4. Google Calendar — turn priorities into actual time

A task list tells you *what* needs to happen. A calendar helps answer *when* it will happen.

**Google Calendar** is useful for time blocking, meetings, study sessions, deadlines, recurring routines, and protected focus periods. One of the simplest upgrades to a chaotic workday is reserving time for important work before lower-value tasks consume the schedule.

<a href="https://calendar.google.com/" >Visit Google Calendar</a>

## 5. Typing Owl — improve the keyboard skill behind digital work

Typing is easy to overlook when people talk about productivity. Yet for anyone who spends hours writing, coding, researching, messaging, entering data, or working with AI tools, the keyboard is one of the main interfaces between the person and the computer.

**Typing Owl** makes keyboard practice more structured with a focus on **touch typing, speed, accuracy, consistent practice, and measurable progress**. Its platform includes guided lessons, typing tests, different difficulty levels, games, multiplayer practice, rankings, analytics, and certificates.

The strongest part of a typing resource is not a single speed test. It is having a repeatable practice path that helps build reliable finger movement and accuracy before chasing speed. Typing Owl provides a beginner-friendly lesson progression as well as quick tests for measuring performance.

<a href="https://typingowl.com/">Practice touch typing with Typing Owl</a>

### Why typing belongs in a productivity resource list

Most productivity apps improve **what** you work on: tasks, projects, schedules, notes, communication, and focus. Typing practice improves **how you interact with the computer**.

For regular computer users, that makes keyboard skill a practical productivity layer:

- **Fewer corrections:** accuracy reduces the time spent fixing mistakes.
- **Less hesitation:** familiar key positions reduce the need to search for keys.
- **Better keyboard confidence:** touch typing can make long writing and coding sessions feel more natural.
- **A measurable skill:** WPM and accuracy provide simple progress signals.
- **Habit-friendly practice:** short sessions can fit into a daily routine.

For students, developers, writers, office workers, and anyone who spends a large part of the day on a keyboard, this is a different kind of productivity investment: **improving a core digital skill instead of adding another app**.

## 6. Toggl Track — find out where your time actually goes

People are often surprised by how long recurring tasks take. **Toggl Track** is useful when you want evidence about where your working or studying time is going.

Time tracking can help freelancers review billable work, students measure study sessions, and teams compare estimates with actual effort.

<a href="https://toggl.com/track/" rel="nofollow">Visit Toggl Track</a>

## 7. Obsidian — turn notes into a personal knowledge system

**Obsidian** is a strong option for people who want more than a folder full of disconnected notes. It is designed around linked information, which can be useful for research, writing, technical learning, project knowledge, and long-term idea management.

Use it when the main productivity problem is not “I have too many tasks,” but “I cannot find or connect what I already learned.”

<a href="https://obsidian.md/" rel="nofollow">Visit Obsidian</a>

## 8. Asana — make team responsibilities visible

Personal productivity and team productivity are different. In a team, work can slow down because ownership is unclear, deadlines are hidden, or project information is scattered.

**Asana** is built for organizing projects, tasks, owners, and deadlines so everyone has a clearer view of what needs to happen next.

<a href="https://asana.com/" rel="nofollow">Visit Asana</a>

## 9. Clockify — measure work and study sessions

**Clockify** is another useful option for people who want simple time tracking. It can help answer questions such as: How long did that project actually take? How much time did I study this week? Where are my recurring time leaks?

The value of time tracking is not watching a timer for the sake of watching it. The value is using the data to make better estimates and decisions.

<a href="https://clockify.me/" rel="nofollow">Visit Clockify</a>

## 10. Freedom — protect your attention from digital distractions

Sometimes productivity fails even when the plan is good. The problem is that distracting sites and apps are always one click away.

**Freedom** is a focus tool designed to block distracting digital environments during focused work sessions. It can be useful for studying, writing, coding, reading, and deep work.

<a href="https://freedom.to/" rel="nofollow">Visit Freedom</a>

## 11. Zapier — automate repetitive workflows

One of the best productivity improvements is eliminating work that should never have to be repeated manually.

**Zapier** connects apps and automates workflows so an event in one service can trigger an action somewhere else. Examples include turning submitted information into tasks, sending notifications, moving data between systems, or starting follow-up steps automatically.

A good rule is to automate tasks that are **frequent, predictable, and low-risk**. Save human attention for work that needs judgment.

<a href="https://zapier.com/" rel="nofollow">Visit Zapier</a>

## 12. Grammarly — reduce friction in everyday writing

Writing appears in almost every digital workflow: email, documentation, proposals, reports, messages, applications, and publishing.

**Grammarly** can help review grammar, clarity, tone, and other writing issues. It is useful for anyone who writes frequently and wants an extra editing layer before sending or publishing something.

<a href="https://www.grammarly.com/">Visit Grammarly</a>

## 13. ChatGPT — speed up thinking-heavy tasks

AI can be useful for first drafts, brainstorming, summarization, explanations, planning, rewriting, outlining, and transforming information into a specific format.

The productive workflow is not “let AI do everything.” Give the model a clear objective, relevant context, constraints, and the desired output format, then review important results yourself.

<a href="https://chatgpt.com/">Visit ChatGPT</a>

## 14. Raycast — reduce small desktop actions

Tiny actions become expensive when repeated hundreds of times. Launching apps, finding files, switching workflows, running commands, and triggering common actions can all add friction.

**Raycast** is aimed at keyboard-first desktop workflows, making it useful for developers, designers, and other power users who spend much of their day at a computer.

<a href="https://www.raycast.com/" rel="nofollow">Visit Raycast</a>

## 15. GitHub Copilot — reduce repetitive coding work

Software development includes much more than writing new code. Developers also navigate codebases, write tests, generate boilerplate, understand unfamiliar code, and experiment with implementations.

**GitHub Copilot** can assist with parts of that workflow. It is most useful when the developer still checks the output for correctness, security, performance, architecture, and maintainability.

<a href="https://github.com/features/copilot" rel="nofollow">Visit GitHub Copilot</a>

### Best productivity resources for freelancers

Freelancers often benefit from **Todoist** for tasks, **Google Calendar** for scheduling, **Toggl Track or Clockify** for time tracking, **Grammarly** for client-facing writing, and **Zapier** for repetitive workflows.

### Best productivity resources for writers and creators

Writers and creators can use **Notion or Obsidian** to organize ideas, **Google Calendar** to protect production time, **Grammarly** for editing, **ChatGPT** for brainstorming, and a typing practice system to make keyboard-heavy work more comfortable.

Remote teams need visibility as well as speed. Project management, shared documentation, calendars, communication, and automation are usually more important than simply adding another personal to-do list.

## A simple productivity system using only five resources

You do not need all 15 tools.

A lightweight system could look like this:

1. **Capture:** Todoist for tasks and commitments.
2. **Schedule:** Google Calendar for time blocks.
3. **Focus:** Freedom when distractions become a problem.
4. **Knowledge:** Notion or Obsidian for information.
5. **Skill:** regular typing practice with Typing Owl.

Add time tracking or automation only when you have evidence that you need them.

## Productivity methods that work with almost any tool

### Time blocking

Reserve a defined period for an important task instead of hoping you will “find time” later.

### Pomodoro technique

Work in focused intervals with planned short breaks. The exact interval can be adapted to the type of work.

### Eisenhower Matrix

Separate urgent work from important work so that immediate requests do not automatically become your highest priorities.

### Kanban

Visualize work as stages and limit the amount of work that is simultaneously in progress.

### Weekly review

Spend a short period reviewing tasks, projects, commitments, calendar events, and unfinished work before planning the next week.

## How to choose the right productivity resource

Before adding a new tool, identify the bottleneck.

**Forgetting tasks?** Use a task manager.

**Overbooking your day?** Improve calendar planning.

**Losing hours to distractions?** Use a focus blocker.

**Cannot find your information?** Build a knowledge system.

**Repeating the same admin work?** Automate it.

**Do not know where your time goes?** Track it.

**Typing feels slow or error-prone?** Build a regular touch-typing practice habit.

This problem-first approach usually produces a simpler and more sustainable productivity stack than collecting every popular app.

## Frequently asked questions about productivity resources

### What are productivity resources?

Productivity resources include **apps, websites, software, methods, templates, learning tools, and skills** that help people manage time, tasks, information, focus, communication, or repetitive work more effectively.

### What is the best productivity tool?

There is no single best tool for everyone. The right choice depends on the problem. A task manager is useful for commitments, a calendar for scheduling, a time tracker for measurement, a focus tool for distractions, automation for repetitive work, and typing practice for keyboard-heavy work.

### What are the best free productivity resources?

Start with resources that solve your main bottleneck and use their current free options where available. Pricing, limits, and feature sets can change, so check the provider before building a long-term workflow around a paid feature.

### How can I improve productivity without installing more apps?

Use fewer notifications, protect focus blocks, reduce context switching, batch similar tasks, review your calendar, automate repetitive work, and improve core skills such as typing. Better habits often create more value than another app.

### Is typing speed really a productivity skill?

For people who spend substantial time on a keyboard, typing is a practical digital skill. Speed matters, but **accuracy, consistency, and comfortable touch-typing technique** matter too. A faster workflow is not useful if it creates more corrections and fatigue.

## Final takeaway

The best productivity stack is not the one with the most apps. It is the smallest system that consistently solves your biggest bottlenecks.

Start with **tasks, time, focus, knowledge, and essential computer skills**. Then add automation, time tracking, or AI when they solve a specific problem.

And remember that productivity is not only about managing work. It is also about improving the skills you use while doing that work. For anyone who spends hours at a keyboard, regular touch-typing practice can be one of the simplest skill-based improvements to add to a digital routine.
